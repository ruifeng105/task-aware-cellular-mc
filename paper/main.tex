\begin{abstract}
Chemical commands delivered to a living receiver can change how it responds to later commands. Using public experimental FGF2--ERK single-cell trajectories rather than artificial bit labels, we test whether measured stimulation history improves prediction of a cellular receiver's reporter and whether such information could shorten repeated use. A provenance-checked pilot reduced the 10-min-ahead root mean squared error (RMSE) by 8.25\% relative to a current-input/response/clock baseline on an already inspected test. A pre-registered expansion to 14 unused conditions (22 conditions, 1,565 cells) confirmed an 8.8\% gain over that baseline on an unseen 10-min-pulse protocol, but only 0.7\% over naive persistence. We then executed the original authors' B3 biochemical model from their files, reproducing their posterior predictions to within $10^{-5}$. In a post hoc reference comparison, a matched-feedback B3 forecaster alone only equalled persistence, whereas adding causal-history features to it reduced the 10-min RMSE on the unseen protocol by a further 8.2\%, at four of four concentrations. Finally, we define task-dependent receiver readiness and, in simulation on B3, find that after 25~ng/ml commands readiness is nearly a function of the current reporter, the candidate waits of 6--48~min never suffice, and a history-aware waiting policy reaches 90\% success about 8~min faster than the matching fixed wait, mainly by filtering noisy observations. The results support history- and mechanism-informed prediction, not a new biological mechanism or measured task reliability.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
An application of molecular communication (MC) can require a chemical signal to produce a specified response in a living cell. Successful reception then means more than recovering a symbol: the receiver must execute the requested response at the right time. A previous exposure may influence the next response through transport persistence, receptor dynamics, intracellular feedback, or changes in cellular state. For a sequence of commands, an operational question is therefore when the receiver can be used again, and at what chemical and temporal cost.

We ground this question in recorded biology rather than artificial labels: FGF2 stimulation of PC-12 cells, with ERK activity measured by a FRET reporter \cite{blum2019}; ERK stimulation patterns are known to shape cellular outcomes \cite{ryu2015}. The reporter is treated as a continuous receiver output, and no alphabet or cell-fate label is assigned retroactively.

Prior work bounds what a generic learning-based controller could claim. The FGF2 data were originally used to infer signaling architecture, including receptor interactions and feedback \cite{blum2019}; their nontrivial washout response is established biology, not a newly discovered memory mechanism, and frequency-dependent ERK responses are also known \cite{ryu2015}. Postiglione \emph{et al.} controlled mammalian gene expression and signaling with automated chemical delivery and feedback \cite{postiglione2018}, and Lugagne \emph{et al.} used a learned dynamical model for model predictive control (MPC) of thousands of single cells \cite{lugagne2024}. Nonlinear prediction, response feedback and MPC are therefore substantial precedents; the extension considered here is the feasibility and cost of a \emph{next task conditioned on previous tasks}, not chemical feedback control itself.

Within MC, semantic systems already learn task-relevant representations \cite{cheng2024,cai2025}, interpretable reaction--diffusion learning has been proposed \cite{dong2026}, experimental yeast pheromone links characterize biological receiver noise and detection \cite{ntetsikas2025}, genetic-circuit receivers are designed computationally \cite{durmaz2024}, Barros and Dey control calcium signaling and its refractory behavior \cite{barros2020}, CELLEC adapts error control to modeled cellular noise \cite{borges2024}, and long-term experimental MC shows that reuse and resource tradeoffs require explicit engineering \cite{scherer2025}. The candidate distinction to test is a calibrated, sequence-level exposure/time--reliability tradeoff attributable to receiver history beyond a matched-information mechanistic estimator.

The narrower question is therefore whether a measured chemical-response history improves prediction beyond accessible present-state information, and whether that information can eventually reduce exposure or completion time for \emph{repeated tasks at matched reliability}. This draft makes four bounded contributions: (i) an audited experimental input--response pilot with a pre-registered expansion that supplies unseen protocols; (ii) a quantification of causal-history features against current-state models and naive persistence; (iii) an execution of the original B3 model, accepted against the authors' exports, that provides a matched-feedback mechanistic reference and a nested history-residual comparison; and (iv) a task-dependent readiness criterion with a controller specification, examined in simulation on B3. No result concerns measured task reliability.

\section{Cellular MC Model and Task Readiness}
\subsection{Actuation, Delivery, and Observation}
Let $u(t)\geq0$ denote the commanded FGF2 concentration in ng/ml. Delivery to a receiver is represented abstractly as
\begin{equation}
 c_R(t)=\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}[u](t),\quad
 \dot{x}_j=f_{\theta_j}(x_j,c_R),\quad
 y_j=g(x_j)+\nu_j,
 \label{eq:system}
\end{equation}
where $c_R$ is the local concentration, $x_j$ the unobserved state of cell $j$, and $y_j$ its normalized reporter. The operator $\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}$ can include delivery lag and washout; neither it nor $c_R$ is calibrated here. The FRET ratio is not a molecular count, receptor occupancy, or cell-fate measurement. For deployment, $\Hist_t$ contains executed commands and observations whose feedback has \emph{arrived} by time $t$; the offline analysis assumes each processed observation is available at its recorded time, an assumption a live experiment must replace with logged latency.

\subsection{Task-Dependent Readiness: Proposed Definition}
A prospective command $m$ specifies an output band $\mathcal{Y}_m(t)$, a hold interval with acquisition times $\mathcal{I}_m$, a deadline, and any exclusion interval for false activation, all fixed by a separately calibrated application. At the measurement resolution, task success is
\begin{equation}
 S_m=\ind\Big\{\textstyle\bigcap_{t_i\in\mathcal{I}_m}\{y_j(t_i)\in\mathcal{Y}_m(t_i)\}\cap\mathcal{C}_m\Big\},
 \label{eq:task}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{C}_m$ is the event that deadline and exclusion requirements hold. Individual-cell and population criteria differ and must be chosen before a policy is evaluated. For a candidate future command sequence $\mathbf a$, readiness is
\begin{equation}
 R_t(m,\mathbf a)=\Prob(S_m=1\mid\Hist_t,\mathbf a).
 \label{eq:ready}
\end{equation}
Readiness is specific to a requested response and deadline: returning to a reporter baseline need not imply readiness for every next task, and successful delivery may not require complete biochemical reset. These are hypotheses to evaluate. The present data have no prespecified $\mathcal{Y}_m$ or task labels, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:task} is not applied retrospectively, and a point predictor's RMSE is not a calibrated estimate of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ready}.

\section{History-Aware Prediction}
We fit separate predictors for horizons $h=2$ and $10$ min. All learned predictors receive the same known future command: its exposure in successive 2-min bins, the concentration at $t+h$, and the horizon. Future reporter values are targets only, so this is conditional forecasting under recorded protocols, not evaluation of counterfactual commands. The input--response (IR) set contains $u_t$, $u_t^2$, $y_t$, $y_t^2$, $u_ty_t$ and the future-command features; comparators add the clock $t,t^2$ or the slope $(y_t-y_{t-2})/2$. The causal-history set adds this slope, the past exposure $\int_0^t u(s)\,ds$, and input and response filters with scales $\tau\in\{2,10,60\}$ min,
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 q_{\tau}(t)&=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_0^t e^{-(t-s)/\tau}u(s)\,ds,\\
 r_{\tau,i}&=e^{-\Delta_i/\tau}r_{\tau,i-1}+(1-e^{-\Delta_i/\tau})y_i,
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:filters}
\end{equation}
with $r_{\tau,0}=y_0$ and $\Delta_i=t_i-t_{i-1}$; $q_\tau$ is evaluated exactly for the piecewise-constant command. The scales are computational choices, not identified rate constants. A history-plus-clock set adds $t,t^2$ to the history features, giving a nested ablation of the IR-plus-clock comparator. Prediction starts after a fixed 10-min observed warmup; pre-export biological history is unknown.

Let $d$ index training condition blocks with $N_d$ rows each. Row weights $w_i=1/(D_{\rm tr}N_{d(i)})$ give every block equal weight. With features standardized by weighted training statistics, standardized row $a_i$ and weighted target mean $\bar y$,
\begin{equation}
 \hat\beta_{\lambda}=\arg\min_{\beta}\sum_{i\in\mathrm{train}}w_i(y_{i+h}-\bar y-a_i^\top\beta)^2+\lambda\|\beta\|_2^2,
 \label{eq:ridge}
\end{equation}
with the unpenalized intercept $\bar y$. The penalty is selected by condition-equal validation MSE, separately per predictor and horizon; validation blocks are never added to training. The small feature set tests whether history carries usable information before a more complex architecture is justified.

As a mechanistic reference we execute the original authors' B3 model with their 1,000 posterior parameter samples (Sec.~\ref{sec:data}). With $f_s(t)$ the noise-free reporter of sample $s$ under the recorded command, the matched-feedback forecaster M2 weights the samples by each cell's past reporter,
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 w_s(t)&\propto\textstyle\exp\big[-\sum_{t_i\le t}(y_i-f_s(t_i))^2/(2\sigma^2)\big],\\
 \hat y_{t+h}&=y_t+\textstyle\sum_s w_s(t)\,[f_s(t+h)-f_s(t)],
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:b3}
\end{equation}
a fixed-parameter particle approximation of the B3 belief that uses the same information as the learned predictors. M3 adds the M2 increment as one feature to the causal-history ridge, so it nests both causal history (M1) and M2; IR plus clock is M0. The scale $\sigma$ is selected on validation from seven values. The samples express population-mean uncertainty, not cell heterogeneity.

\section{Proposed Receiver-Reuse Controller}
The proposed extension uses actions $a_k=(A_k,d_k,w_k)$: command amplitude, pulse duration, and waiting/washout time, within an experimentally calibrated set $\calA$ that also encodes actuator bounds, task order and deadlines. Chemical washout and cellular recovery can differ and should be measured separately. When cells share a chamber and one command, the policy must optimize a prespecified population criterion. For ordered tasks with $S_{\rm seq}=\prod_{k=1}^K S_{m_k}$, a candidate planning problem is
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \min_{\mathbf a\in\calA}\ &\eta_E\E[E(\mathbf a)\mid\Hist_t]+\eta_T\E[T(\mathbf a)\mid\Hist_t]\\
 \text{s.t.}\ &\Prob(S_{\rm seq}=1\mid\Hist_t,\mathbf a)\geq1-\epsilon .
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:mpc}
\end{equation}
The constraint concerns the joint sequence event; multiplying marginal success probabilities would silently assume conditional independence across a reused receiver. The exposure $E=\int u(t)\,dt$ (ng$\cdot$min/ml) is only a proxy: delivered mass requires calibrated flow $Q(t)$ via $\int Q(t)c_{\rm delivered}(t)\,dt$, and molecule numbers further require a delivery model. A rollout model may be a mechanistic state estimator, a learned state model, or a learned residual; its distribution and task-event calibration must use separate data containing the relevant actions and outcomes.

The controller executes the first action of the solution, logs actuation and delivery, and replans after feedback; without a feasible sequence it keeps a reference wait rule. Its waiting-only special case probes once the estimated success probability reaches a level $q$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:example}, Sec.~\ref{sec:readiness}). On measured receivers it requires calibrated delivery, prespecified tasks, an action-supported rollout model and joint-event calibration.

\section{Experimental Data and Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:data}
\subsection{Source, Audit, and Pilot Split}
All numerical files come from the paper-linked \texttt{Mijan/LFNS\_MSB} repository at pinned commit \texttt{5c917ab} \cite{lfnsrepo}; the associated data record is cited for provenance \cite{mendeley2020}. Every file used here matches its Git blob identifier. Commands follow the authors' model XML and pulse annotations with half-open intervals: 3/20 pulses are 3~min on every 23~min, single pulses start at 0, and mixed pulses span $[1,4)$, $[24,54)$ and $[114,119)$~min. For the mixed protocol we reconstructed the 10--30~min per-cell median normalization from unnormalized exports (maximum discrepancy $1.4\times10^{-4}$, export rounding); other conditions retain author preprocessing. The pilot trained on the sustained and 3/20 blocks at 2.5 and 250~ng/ml, validated on single 5-min pulses, and tested on mixed pulses (606 cells, 53,998 observations). That test has since been inspected and serves only as context.

\subsection{Pre-Registered Expansion}
Before downloading any newly used response file, we froze an analysis protocol (roles, schedules, features, penalty grid $\{10^{-8},\ldots,10^{4}\}$, horizons, metrics, hypotheses and data rules) and recorded its SHA-256 hash; the analysis refuses to run if the protocol changes, and it was run once. The 56 additional files all matched their upstream blob identifiers. Table~\ref{tab:inventory} lists the 22 conditions. Training uses the sustained and 3/20 blocks at all concentrations, validation the single 5-min pulses. Test A is a new protocol (single 10-min pulse, timing from the XML). Test B is the single 60-min pulse; no XML input exists, so its $[0,60)$ timing was inferred from the authors' B3 simulations, which match their sustained simulations through 60~min and diverge by 64--78~min, with a pre-registered $\pm1$~min onset sensitivity; their simulation summary later confirmed this timing. Test C uses the mixed protocol at new concentrations; raw file names show it was recorded as further channels of the inspected experiment, so it shares that session. The primary hypotheses compare causal history (H1) and history plus clock (H2) with the IR-plus-clock comparator at 10~min on Test A. All cells passed the rules (uniform 2-min grids, finite values). For 0.25~ng/ml the exported ``unnormalized'' file is identical to the normalized one, so its normalization check is uninformative; at 25~ng/ml the discrepancy is $1.4\times10^{-4}$.

\subsection{B3 Execution and Reference Protocol}
B3 is executed by parsing the authors' model, initial-value and measurement files, with the posterior order taken from their simulation summary; no equation is retyped and no parameter is refit. An acceptance gate fixed in advance required our posterior-predictive mean to match their exported mixed-protocol predictions within $10^{-3}$: the maximum differences were $7.9\times10^{-6}$ and $8.5\times10^{-6}$, sample-wise errors equal their added measurement noise ($10^{-4}$), and conservation laws hold to about $10^{-14}$. B3 was fitted to sustained and 3/20 means at 2.5 and 250~ng/ml, and mixed and single 5-min conditions at those doses entered its architecture selection. The M0--M3 comparison was frozen in its own protocol before any B3 forecast was scored; because all roles had been inspected for the learned models, it is a post hoc reference analysis.

\subsection{Scores and Comparators}
The score is the square root of the equally weighted condition MSE,
\begin{equation}
 \mathrm{RMSE}_{\rm cond}(h)=\Big[\tfrac{1}{|D|}\textstyle\sum_{d\in D}\tfrac{1}{N_{d,h}}\sum_{i\in d}(\hat y_{i+h}-y_{i+h})^2\Big]^{1/2},
 \label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
reported with per-condition directions. Overlapping rows and cells are not independent interventions, and experimental-day identifiers are unavailable, so no significance test or confidence interval is reported. Persistence ($\hat y_{t+h}=y_t$) is included because it needs no training.

\subsection{Mechanistic Reference and Nested History Residual}
Table~\ref{tab:expanded} also lists the B3 forecasters ($\sigma=0.005$ at both horizons). Without feedback, the B3 posterior-mean forecast is far worse (0.03819 on Test A). With matched feedback, M2 beats IR plus clock (0.02286 versus 0.02490) but only equals persistence (0.02287) and is marginally behind causal history (0.02270). Adding the history features to the M2 increment (M3) gives the lowest RMSE in every role at both horizons: 0.02098 on Test A, 8.2\% below M2 and 8.3\% below persistence, ahead of M2 at four of four concentrations; on Tests B and C the reductions relative to M2 are 0.3\% and 4.9\%. Alone, neither the mechanistic estimator nor the history summary improves on persistence by more than 0.7\% on the unseen protocol; only their combination does. This is a reference comparison on inspected roles, not a fresh test.

\subsection{In-Silico Readiness and Waiting on B3}
\label{sec:readiness}
Readiness and waiting policies were then simulated on B3 under a protocol frozen before running (Fig.~\ref{fig:readiness}). A 3- or 30-min command at 25~ng/ml ends at time 0 and a 5-min probe follows after a wait; the task is an incremental response of at least half the same sample's response on an unstimulated receiver within 20~min. Each posterior sample acts in turn as the plant, observed every 2~min with noise (s.d. 0.005), while controllers use the other 999 samples. A first version requiring 80\% of the naive response was infeasible for every sample within 120~min (largest recovered fraction 0.79) and is reported unchanged in the package; the version shown changes only that threshold and two rules based on baseline return, which never occurred (a reset policy and the history comparison). Readiness rose only after about 60~min and reached 0.5 at 78 and 88~min after short and long histories, so the candidate waits of 6--48~min never succeeded. Although the reporter never returned within 0.01 of baseline, readiness depended almost only on its current value: nearly all states below a rise of 0.045 were ready and almost none above 0.050, for both histories (mean difference 0.6 percentage points). Weighting the other samples by all observations and probing at $q=0.9$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:example}) achieved 90.0\% success with a mean failure-penalized completion time of 113.8~min, against 121.8~min for the shortest fixed wait with at least that success (100~min, 91.3\%). Using only the current observation gave 75.6\% at 119.7~min, a half-decay reset 2.2\%, and an oracle 99.3\% at 104.9~min. Because readiness is nearly Markov in the reporter, the history-aware advantage here comes from filtering noisy observations rather than hidden memory; the controllers share the plant's model structure, and none of this is evidence about measured cells.

\subsection{Command-Only Models and Mechanistic Reference}
For population means without reporter feedback, a positive single-lag model (validation time constant 13.16~min) and a ridge filter bank with scales 1--128~min were fit on the pilot split. On 0--180~min (Table~\ref{tab:openloop}), the heavily regularized bank improves on a constant training mean by only 0.9\% and 2.7\%, and the single lag is worse than the constant; the mean of the original authors' 1,000 B3 predictive samples is much closer. B3 used mixed and single-pulse conditions in its architecture selection, so it is a selection-exposed reference, not a blind benchmark, and population-mean errors are not comparable with the individual forecasts above. After the long pulse the mean reporter still rises from 54 to 66~min with zero command, which a nonnegative single lag cannot reproduce; the source biology reports such washout behavior \cite{blum2019}.

\subsection{Exploratory Matched History}
Pairing early washout after the short mixed pulse (6--12~min) with later washout after the long pulse (56--102~min) at similar reporter values (tolerance 0.005), the later-minus-earlier 10-min target is positive at 2.5~ng/ml (60/78 cells, $+0.0144$; slope-matched 14/78, $+0.0202$) but negative at 250~ng/ml, shrinking to $-0.0012$ when slope-matched (17/76). These post hoc, post-stimulation matches identify no causal history effect.

\section{Discussion and Required Validation}
The question is molecular because the task involves nonnegative chemical actuation, delivery and washout dynamics, a nonlinear receiver, and history-dependent availability: waiting is a control action with a time cost, and exposure has its own cost. Fresh protocols confirm that history features beat a current-state-plus-clock model; against persistence they help mainly when combined with a mechanistic estimator or when training covers diverse stimulation, and the long mixed protocol exposes clock extrapolation. In B3, readiness after 25~ng/ml commands takes over an hour and is nearly determined by the current reporter, so the registered candidate waits should be extended and the expected value of history lies in state estimation under noise; whether measured cells carry history beyond the reporter remains open. None of this separates delivery memory from intracellular memory or establishes task reliability.

A draft validation registration accompanies the package: 3- versus 30-min preconditioning randomized per chamber/session, a common 5-min probe at a fixed protocol clock (90 or 120~min) after 6--48~min waits, and matched controls, with uncalibrated candidate doses and times. The primary comparison is a mechanistic state estimator plus a history residual against the same estimator with identical feedback (group-level Brier score of the next task event); the primary endpoint is the failure-penalized completion time $S\,T+(1-S)D$ of two ordered tasks completed by the same eligible cells, starting with adaptive waiting. Sample sizes count independent days or chambers, never cells: zero-failure support for 0.95 sequence reliability needs 59 independent sequences. A targeted audit of 18 neighboring works, with recorded full-text gaps, supports no priority claim. If history adds no independent-day benefit, or a mechanistic estimator reaches the same reliability and cost, the claim should be narrowed.

\section{Conclusion}
On experimental FGF2--ERK trajectories, causal-history features improved 10-min prediction over a current-state-plus-clock model in a pilot and in a pre-registered test on unseen protocols, and combined with a matched-feedback execution of the authors' B3 model they beat naive persistence by 8.3\% in a post hoc comparison. In simulation on B3, task readiness tracked the current reporter, and a history-aware waiting policy met a 90\% reliability target faster than fixed waiting by filtering noisy observations. Demonstrating such benefits for measured receivers requires calibrated delivery, prespecified tasks, supported interventions and new independent-session experiments.
