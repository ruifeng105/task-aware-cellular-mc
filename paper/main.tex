\begin{abstract}
Using public experimental FGF2--ERK single-cell trajectories, we ask which information predicts a cellular receiver's response to repeated chemical commands and whether it can shorten repeated use. In nested post hoc comparisons on a protocol unseen in training, reporter filtering reduced the 10-min-ahead error of a current-state predictor by 9.1\%, a matched-feedback execution of the authors' B3 model reduced the filtered predictor's error by 4.5\%, and explicit input-history features reduced the mechanistic predictor's error by 1.0\%, ending 8.2\% below persistence. With predictor tuning restricted to the training protocols and a fixed, previously calibrated B3 reference, explicit input history added 0.1--2.0\% in all six leave-one-protocol-out folds. For a fixed probe task we define readiness and, in simulation on B3, calibrate waiting rules on half of the posterior samples and evaluate them on the other half. A history-aware rule completed 7.4~min faster than the calibrated fixed wait, and was faster in all 20 repeated splits, but a smoothed reporter did as well. In this B3 setting the gain reflects filtering of noisy observations; no result concerns measured task reliability.
\end{abstract}

Molecular communication, cellular receivers, mechanism-informed prediction, receiver reuse, waiting rules, FGF2, ERK.

\section{Introduction}
Molecular communication (MC) with living receivers asks a chemical command to produce a specified response in a cell. Reception then means executing the requested response on time, and a previous command can change the next response through delivery persistence, receptor dynamics, intracellular feedback, or cellular state. For repeated tasks the operational question is when the receiver can be used again at matched reliability. We ground this question in recorded biology: FGF2 stimulation of PC-12 cells with ERK activity read by a FRET reporter \cite{blum2019}, treated as a continuous receiver output without retroactive symbols or cell-fate labels.

Two lines of work bound the claim. In cell dynamics, the FGF2 data were used to infer signaling architecture, including feedback and a nontrivial washout response \cite{blum2019}; stimulation frequency shapes ERK responses \cite{ryu2015}; automated chemical feedback has controlled mammalian signaling \cite{postiglione2018}; and a learned dynamical model enabled model predictive control of thousands of single cells \cite{lugagne2024}. In MC, memory is classical: diffusion and ligand binding produce intersymbol interference and reception noise \cite{farsad2016,jamali2019,pierobon2011}, which guard intervals, ISI-aware modulation and adaptive threshold detection mitigate \cite{tepekule2015,damrath2016}. Semantic systems learn task-relevant representations \cite{cheng2024,cai2025}, interpretable reaction--diffusion learning has been proposed \cite{dong2026}, experimental yeast pheromone links characterize biological receiver noise \cite{ntetsikas2025}, genetic-circuit receivers are designed computationally \cite{durmaz2024}, calcium signaling with refractory behavior has been controlled \cite{barros2020}, error control has been adapted to cellular noise \cite{borges2024}, and long-term experimental MC exposes reuse tradeoffs \cite{scherer2025}. Prediction, feedback and predictive control are therefore not new; the open question is which information makes a \emph{next task} predictable for a living receiver, whose memory lies in its biochemistry rather than only in the channel, and how much a waiting decision gains from it.

This paper makes two bounded contributions. (i) A nested comparison quantifies the contributions of reporter filtering, a calibrated mechanistic increment from the authors' B3 model, and explicit input-history features to experimental FGF2--ERK forecasting, with phase-wise errors and leave-one-protocol-out (LOPO) validation in which predictor tuning is restricted to the training protocols. (ii) For a fixed probe task we define readiness and compare calibrated waiting rules in simulation on B3, with a smoothed-reporter baseline, a noise ablation and repeated calibration/evaluation splits. Prediction and waiting gains are evaluated separately, and no result concerns measured task reliability.

\section{System and Task Model}
Let $u(t)\geq0$ denote the commanded FGF2 concentration in ng/ml. Delivery to a receiver is represented abstractly as
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 c_R(t)&=\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}[u](t),\quad \dot{x}_j=f_{\theta_j}(x_j,c_R),\\
 z_j&=g(x_j),\quad y_{j,i}=z_j(t_i)+\nu_{j,i},
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:system}
\end{equation}
where $c_R$ is the local concentration, $x_j$ the unobserved state of cell $j$, $z_j$ its noise-free normalized reporter, and $y_{j,i}$ the observation with noise $\nu_{j,i}$ at frame $t_i$. The operator $\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}$ can include delivery lag and washout; neither it nor $c_R$ is calibrated here, and the FRET ratio is not a molecular count or a cell-fate measurement. The available information $\Hist_t$ contains the executed commands and the observations whose feedback has arrived by $t$; offline, each observation is assumed available at its recorded time.

We fix the amplitude and duration of the next command and decide only when to send it (Fig.~\ref{fig:system}). After a previous command ending at time 0, a 5-min probe at the same amplitude is sent at $\tau\in\mathcal{T}=\{0,2,\ldots,120\}$~min. With $z_j^{\tau}$ the noise-free response when the probe is sent at $\tau$ after the recorded history, and $r_{{\rm naive},j}$ the same receiver's rise to the probe without a previous command, the task outcome and readiness are
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 S_j(\tau)&=\ind\Big\{\max_{s\in[\tau,\tau+W]}z_j^{\tau}(s)-z_j^{\tau}(\tau)\geq\kappa\,r_{{\rm naive},j}\Big\},\\
 R_{j,t}&=\Prob\big(S_j(t)=1\mid\Hist_t\big),
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:task}
\end{equation}
with task window $W=20$~min and $\kappa=0.5$. Observation noise affects the feedback used for decision-making; task success is scored from the noise-free model response, and decisions use only observations available by $\tau$. The counterfactual $r_{{\rm naive},j}$ is a simulation benchmark; an experiment would have to measure or estimate it, or replace it by a fixed measurable threshold. The completion time is $T=\tau+W$ on success and the deadline penalty $D=140$~min on failure; the design objective is to minimize $\E[T]$ subject to $\Prob(S=1)\geq q=0.9$, an empirical target rather than a coverage guarantee. Readiness is task-specific: a reporter need not return to baseline before the receiver is ready for this task. The experimental data have no task labels, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:task} is evaluated only in simulation.

\section{Mechanism-Informed Prediction and Waiting}
\subsection{Nested Predictors}
Separate predictors are fit for horizons $h=2$ and 10~min after a fixed 10-min observed warmup. Every learned predictor receives the same known future command (its exposure in 2-min bins, the concentration at $t+h$, and $h$); future reporters are targets only. The current-state set C contains $u_t$, $u_t^2$, $y_t$, $y_t^2$, $u_ty_t$, the slope $(y_t-y_{t-2})/2$ and the future-command features. Causal filters with scales $\tau\in\{2,10,60\}$~min summarize input and reporter history,
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 q_{\tau}(t)&=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_0^t e^{-(t-s)/\tau}u(s)\,ds,\\
 r_{\tau,i}&=e^{-\Delta_i/\tau}r_{\tau,i-1}+(1-e^{-\Delta_i/\tau})y_i,
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:filters}
\end{equation}
with $r_{\tau,0}=y_0$ and $\Delta_i=t_i-t_{i-1}$; the scales are computational choices, not identified rate constants. O adds the reporter filters $r_\tau$ to C. As a mechanism we execute the authors' B3 model with their 1,000 posterior parameter samples. With $f_s(t)$ the noise-free reporter of sample $s$ under the recorded command, the samples are weighted by each cell's past reporter,
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 w_s(t)&\propto\textstyle\exp\big[-\sum_{t_i\le t}(y_i-f_s(t_i))^2/(2\sigma^2)\big],\\
 \hat\delta_{t,h}&=\textstyle\sum_s w_s(t)\,[f_s(t+h)-f_s(t)],
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:b3}
\end{equation}
a fixed-parameter particle approximation of the B3 belief that uses the same information as the learned predictors. M adds $\hat\delta_{t,h}$ to O, and H adds the input filters $q_\tau$ and the cumulative exposure $\int_0^tu(s)\,ds$ to M. The B3 increment already uses the command history and past observations, so H tests only the additional predictive value of explicit input-history features. Persistence P ($\hat y_{t+h}=y_t$) needs no training. Each set is fit by ridge regression with equal weight per condition and standardization from training rows; the penalty (from $10^{-8}$ to $10^{4}$) and $\sigma$ (seven values) are selected on validation. Adding the clock $t,t^2$ to each set is a sensitivity analysis. The B3 samples express population-mean uncertainty, not cell heterogeneity.

\subsection{Calibrated Waiting Rules}
A waiting rule probes at
\begin{equation}
 \tau=\begin{cases}\inf\{t\in\mathcal{T}:\widehat R_t\geq c\}, & \text{if such a } t \text{ exists},\\ \max\mathcal{T}, & \text{otherwise}.\end{cases}
 \label{eq:wait}
\end{equation}
Three estimators of $R_t$ are compared. The current estimator uses only $y_t$, through a Gaussian kernel over the (reporter, success) states of reference receivers; the smoothed estimator applies the same kernel to a causal exponential smoothing of the observations (scale 2--16~min); the history-aware estimator weights reference receivers under the known command history by all observations so far, as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:b3}. Per-frame probability calibration does not guarantee the reliability of an adaptive stopping rule, so the whole rule is calibrated on calibration receivers and evaluated on separate evaluation receivers. For the current and history estimators we select the first threshold on the prespecified grid $\{0.5,\ldots,0.99\}$ whose calibration success reaches $q$; this is a threshold-selection heuristic guided by the completion-time objective. For each smoothing scale we first select a feasible threshold in the same way, and among feasible scales we choose the one with the smallest calibration mean completion time. If no threshold is feasible, the largest is used and the rule is reported as missing the target. The comparator fixed wait is the shortest grid wait whose calibration success reaches $q$. As a sensitivity analysis, the objective-aligned choice selects, among all feasible thresholds (or scale--threshold pairs, or waits), the one with the smallest calibration mean completion.

\subsection{Mechanism, Validation and Scores}
B3 is executed by parsing the authors' model files; no equation is retyped, no parameter is refit, and our posterior-predictive mean matches their exported predictions within $8.5\times10^{-6}$ (acceptance gate $10^{-3}$). Because the authors fitted B3 to the sustained and 3/20 means and compared architectures on the single 5-min and viewed mixed blocks at 2.5 and 250~ng/ml (Table~\ref{tab:inventory}), M and H use a calibrated reference, not a model blind to those protocols. In LOPO each of six protocol groups is held out in turn, with predictor tuning restricted to the training protocols: $\sigma$ is selected on the training groups, the penalty by inner leave-one-group-out over the training groups, and the held-out group enters no standardization, selection or fit, while B3 remains a fixed, previously calibrated reference. Only the 10- and 60-min pulse protocols were absent from the original B3 fitting and architecture comparison. Forecast rows are labeled by phase: stimulation (command on at $t$), next command (a pulse starts in $(t,t+h]$), early washout (last pulse ended at most 30~min before $t$), and late. After its warmup Test A contains only washout rows, so it mainly tests washout prediction. The score is the square root of the equally weighted condition MSE,
\begin{equation}
 \mathrm{RMSE}_{\rm cond}(h)=\Big[\tfrac{1}{|D|}\textstyle\sum_{d\in D}\tfrac{1}{N_{d,h}}\sum_{i\in d}(\hat y_{i+h}-y_{i+h})^2\Big]^{1/2},
 \label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
reported with per-condition directions. Rows and cells are not independent interventions and experimental-day identifiers are unavailable, so no significance test is reported.

\subsection{Nested Prediction}
Table~\ref{tab:core}(a) reports the nested predictors. On Test A at 10~min, reporter filtering reduced the error by 9.1\% relative to C, the B3 increment by 4.5\% relative to O, and explicit input-history features by 1.0\% relative to M, giving 0.02098, 8.2\% below persistence; these reductions use different baselines and are not additive. Because M already uses the command history and past observations, the H-versus-M gain measures only the added value of explicit input-history features, and it was small and not consistent across conditions: two of four concentrations on Test A, 0.4\% and 0.1\% on B and C, and 0.2\% at 2~min. The mechanistic increment helped in every test role (M versus O: 9.8\% on B and 6.0\% on C), whereas the filtering gain vanished on B and C. Clock terms did not help (0.1--2.4\% worse on the tests), and C alone was worse than persistence on Tests A and B.

\subsection{Forecast Phases}
In Fig.~\ref{fig:prediction}(a) persistence lags the washout by the horizon, whereas M and H follow it (cell RMSE 0.0235, 0.0190 and 0.0189). By phase (Fig.~\ref{fig:prediction}(b)), the mechanistic increment reduced the error of O in every phase except the next-command window (by 8.9\% during stimulation, 4.6\% in early washout and 6.6\% late); in early washout O, M and H reached 0.02919, 0.02785 and 0.02757. During stimulation the reporter filters hurt (O 0.03522 versus C 0.03316), and in the late phase M was best with H slightly worse. The next-command window is covered by two mixed conditions with ten forecast origins each. The B3 increment helped at 0.25~ng/ml (O 0.03652, M 0.03485, H 0.03486) but raised the error at 25~ng/ml (0.02920, 0.03603 and 0.03581), so the pooled error rose from 0.03306 to 0.03544. Across the two mixed conditions contributing to this window, the B3 increment thus increased the aggregate forecast error, which limits its present use for predicting responses to an imminent command despite its benefits during washout.

\subsection{Corrected Leave-One-Protocol-Out}
In the corrected LOPO (Table~\ref{tab:core}(b)), H beat M in all six folds, by 0.1--2.0\%, and beat persistence by 8.0--30.6\%. The B3 increment contributed more than reporter filtering in five folds (up to 21.6\% on 3/20). Of the two protocols absent from the original B3 fitting and architecture comparison, the 60-min pulse benefited from the increment (M 10.8\% below O), whereas with the 10-min pulse held out O was best and M was 1.1\% worse. The sustained and 3/20 folds contain conditions used to fit B3, so their mechanistic gains are not out-of-sample for the mechanism. An earlier LOPO that reused the primary penalties is kept in the package.

\subsection{Calibrated Waiting on B3}
\label{sec:waiting}
Readiness and waiting were simulated on B3 with 25~ng/ml commands, a 3- or 30-min previous command, and each posterior sample in turn as the receiver, observed every 2~min with noise of s.d. 0.005. Readiness depended almost only on the current reporter for both histories (Fig.~\ref{fig:waiting}(a)). A first task version requiring 80\% of the naive response was infeasible for every sample within 120~min and is reported in the package, and the registered candidate waits of 6--48~min never succeeded. On the primary split, with rules calibrated on 500 samples and evaluated on the other 500 (Fig.~\ref{fig:waiting}(c)), the history-aware rule reached 92.6\% success with a mean completion of 114.3~min, faster than the calibrated fixed wait (100~min: 91.2\%, 121.8~min) by 7.4~min (paired 95\% bootstrap interval over evaluation samples, 6.6--8.2~min); the current-observation rule reached 90.0\% at 119.7~min. A smoothed reporter (16-min scale) reached 92.3\% at 113.6~min, 0.7~min faster than the history rule (0.3--1.1~min) with a success difference of 0.3 points ($-1.2$ to 1.8). These pooled values average the two histories, and the fixed wait hides a history dependence (Table~\ref{tab:waiting}): 97.4\% success after the 3-min command but 85.0\% after the 30-min command, whereas both adaptive rules stayed between 91\% and 95\%. Over 20 splits with fresh observation noise (Fig.~\ref{fig:waiting}(d)), the history rule was faster than the fixed wait in every split (by 6.3--7.8~min), the smoothed rule was faster than the history rule in every split (by 0.2--1.0~min), and evaluation success reached 0.9 in 20 splits for both, 19 for the fixed wait and 14 for the current rule. The objective-aligned selection chose the same thresholds, scales and waits as the heuristic in all 40 split--noise combinations. At zero observation noise (Fig.~\ref{fig:waiting}(b)), no candidate history threshold attained the calibration target: calibration success was 67.2\%, and evaluation success under the fallback threshold was 70.1\% (no split reached the target), as the weights collapsed onto essentially one reference sample (median effective sample size 1.1 versus 28.9 with noise); the current rule reached 93.6\% at 113.8~min.

Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity} varies the task threshold and the observation noise on the primary split. At $\kappa=0.4$ and 0.5 the history-aware rule stayed 6.3--7.4~min faster than the calibrated fixed wait at both noise levels. Doubling the noise to 0.01 slowed the current rule beyond the fixed wait (131.0 versus 121.8~min at $\kappa=0.5$), whereas both filtered rules kept their advantage; there the history rule was 1.6--2.6~min faster than the smoothed reporter but 2.6 points less reliable, because the coarse threshold grid placed the two rules at different success levels. At $\kappa=0.6$ even the oracle succeeded for only 81.5\% of samples within 120~min, so no rule could reach the target. In this B3 setting, reporter smoothing matched the history-aware rule at the reference noise level, model-based filtering mattered only at higher noise, and the results do not establish an additional waiting benefit from latent memory; the oracle (99.2\%, 104.7~min) bounds what better state estimation could add.

\subsection{Implications for MC Design}
In MC terms, a previous command acts on the next one like intersymbol interference whose memory sits in the receiver's biochemistry rather than only in the channel \cite{jamali2019,tepekule2015}. A fixed wait is a guard interval that must cover the slowest history: the calibrated 100-min wait succeeded after 97.4\% of 3-min commands but only 85.0\% of 30-min commands (Table~\ref{tab:waiting}), and it lengthened with the task threshold (80 and 100~min for $\kappa=0.4$ and 0.5; at $\kappa=0.6$ no wait up to 120~min sufficed). A feedback-driven wait adapts this interval to the observed receiver state, much as adaptive threshold detection adapts to residual interference \cite{damrath2016}, and saved 6.3--7.4~min per task at similar success in our simulations. As reception noise does for molecular receivers \cite{pierobon2011}, observation noise decides which estimator the feedback needs: a smoothed reporter sufficed at the reference noise, whereas single observations became slower than fixed waiting at doubled noise. A prospective test should therefore randomize 3- versus 30-min preconditioning per chamber, extend probe waits to 60--120~min (the 6--48-min candidates never succeeded in B3), measure the naive response in unpreconditioned controls of the same session, and count independent sessions rather than cells.


\subsection{Evidence Boundaries}
Seven limits qualify these results. First, in the frozen analysis of the main fresh test, history features alone improved on persistence by only 0.7\%, and explicit input-history features add about 1\% beyond the mechanism. Second, the nested and hybrid comparisons are post hoc on inspected roles, not independent confirmation. Third, Test C shares a session with inspected data and no experimental-day identifiers exist, so protocol generalization is not cross-day generalization. Fourth, the waiting results come from B3 simulation: success over posterior samples is not the reliability of a measured cell population, the posterior samples are not validated cell heterogeneity, and the 90\% target carries no coverage guarantee. Fifth, the waiting gain is attributable to filtering in this setting; the zero-noise failure of the history rule reflects finite reference samples and the likelihood bandwidth and says nothing general about biological memory, and whether measured cells carry history that matters for control beyond the reporter remains open. Sixth, the task threshold was lowered from 80\% to 50\% after the first version proved infeasible. Seventh, amplitude and duration were fixed, so nothing is claimed about joint exposure--time optimization.

\section{Conclusion}
On experimental FGF2--ERK trajectories, reporter filtering and a calibrated mechanistic increment from the authors' B3 model account for most of the 10-min prediction gain; explicit input-history features add a small gain, consistent across protocol folds but not conditions, and the mechanism is least reliable before an imminent command. In B3 simulations, calibrated history-aware and smoothed-reporter waiting rules met a 90\% target faster than fixed waiting; the history model added nothing over smoothing at the reference noise and traded speed for reliability at higher noise.
