\begin{abstract}
A living cell used as a molecular receiver remembers earlier commands, so a command sent too early can fail. We ask what information predicts a cell's reporter response under repeated commands and whether feedback can replace a fixed guard interval. On 1,565 experimental FGF2--ERK single-cell trajectories, nested predictors show that a calibrated mechanistic (B3) increment reduces the 10-min forecast error (RMSE) on the validation and all three test protocols (by 4.5--9.8\%) and in five of six held-out protocols, whereas reporter filtering helps on only two protocols and explicit input history adds at most 1\% on the test protocols. In the next-command windows of two test conditions, however, it raises the pooled 10-min error by 7.2\% (through the 25~ng/ml condition; it lowers it at 2, 20 and 30~min), and a later probe pulse in those 25~ng/ml cells is consistent with B3 overestimating recovery after a long command. In B3 simulations with a 90\% reliability requirement for each previous command and reporter noise s.d.\ 0.005, waiting on a smoothed reporter or the B3 belief finishes 5.5--7.7~min earlier than history-conditioned fixed waits and closes 38--45\% of their gap to an oracle across three pre-specified calibration targets (42--45\% at the only target met by every rule in all splits). With a feedback delay the rule must predict readiness ahead; if B3 is the true receiver, the mechanistic estimate pays off, with or without delay, at high reporter noise and with 10-min sampling at s.d.\ 0.005, while a smoothed reporter with its trend suffices otherwise.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{M}{olecular} communication (MC) with living cells as receivers offers a natural interface between engineered communication systems and biological processes. Unlike a conventional receiver, a cell retains the effect of earlier chemical stimuli in receptor dynamics, intracellular signaling and feedback, so repeated commands are not independent: a receiver that has just responded may not execute the next command with the same reliability. This raises a communication-level question for repeated molecular tasks: \emph{when can a living receiver be used again, and what information does that decision need?}

Receiver memory is familiar in MC, but it is usually placed in the channel. Diffusion leaves residual molecules that cause intersymbol interference (ISI) \cite{farsad2016,jamali2019}, and receivers counter it with sequence detection and equalization \cite{kilinc2013}, decoders that exploit memory and sampling rate \cite{mosayebi2014}, adaptive thresholds \cite{damrath2016}, and symbol or detection intervals that trade rate against residual ISI \cite{tepekule2015,cao2020}; ligand-binding reception adds its own noise \cite{pierobon2011}. In these designs the guard interval is set in advance. For a cellular receiver the memory is a hidden biochemical state seen only through a noisy reporter, and deciding from that reporter when to send the next command is a stopping problem, related to sequential detection \cite{wald1945,tung2019} and optimal stopping \cite{poor2009}. History-dependent FGF2--ERK responses \cite{blum2019} and frequency-dependent signaling \cite{ryu2015} show that such memory exists, feedback control of single cells is feasible \cite{postiglione2018,lugagne2024}, and experimental and computational work on biological MC systems is emerging \cite{ntetsikas2025,scherer2025,durmaz2024,barros2020}. What is missing is a measurement-based account of which information predicts a cell's response under repeated commands, including the next one, and a test of whether feedback can replace a fixed guard interval.

We combine experimental FGF2--ERK single-cell trajectories with the calibrated B3 signaling model of \cite{blum2019}. Nested predictors add the current reporter, causal reporter filters, lagged values, a mechanistic B3 increment and explicit input history one at a time. Receiver reuse is posed as a calibrated stopping rule on B3 with a reliability requirement for each previous command. Feedback delay connects the two parts only conceptually: with a delay the rule must predict readiness ahead, as the forecasts do, but it does not use the experimental predictors. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item On measured cells, the B3 increment lowers the 10-min forecast error on the validation and all three test protocols (by 4.5--9.8\% in RMSE) and in five of six held-out protocols, and on Test A lifts the noise-adjusted skill at 20--30~min to 21\% (9--13\% with filtering alone); at 10~min reporter filtering helps on the validation protocol and Test A only, and input history adds at most 1\% in RMSE on the test protocols (up to 2\% across held-out protocols) and destabilizes longer forecasts on Tests A and B. In the next-command windows of two test conditions the B3 increment raised the pooled 10-min error, through the 25~ng/ml condition, and a later probe pulse in those cells is consistent with B3 overestimating recovery after a long command.
\item Under a per-history 90\% reliability requirement at the reference noise (s.d.\ 0.005), waiting on a smoothed reporter or the B3 belief finishes 5.5--7.7~min earlier than history-conditioned fixed waits and closes 38--45\% of their gap to the oracle across three pre-specified calibration targets (42--45\% at 0.94, the only target at which every rule met the requirement in all splits); at this noise the smoothed reporter is as good as the B3 belief, and the raw current reporter gains nothing.
\item A condition map over feedback delay, sampling interval and noise shows when a correct mechanistic model is worth using: readiness must be predicted rather than nowcast once the delay reaches about 20~min, and the B3-predictive rule beats a smoothed reporter with its trend by 0.9--5.5~min at high reporter noise (at similar success in 11 of 15 map cells) and by up to 1.1~min with sparse sampling at the reference noise, but not at low noise or with dense sampling at the reference noise.
\end{itemize}

\section{System and Task Model}
\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig1_system_concept.pdf}
  \caption{System concept. Repeated FGF2 commands drive a living receiver whose biochemical memory shapes the ERK/FRET response $y(t)$ to later commands. The response supports (1) prediction of $\widehat{y}(t+h)$ and (2) readiness-based reuse, where the next command is sent once $\widehat{R}_t\geq c$.}
  \label{fig:system}
\end{figure*}
Let $u(t)\geq0$ be the commanded FGF2 concentration (ng/ml). Delivery to the receiver is represented abstractly as
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \tilde u(t)&=\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}[u](t),\quad \dot{x}_j=f_{\theta_j}(x_j,\tilde u),\\
 z_j&=g(x_j),\quad y_{j,i}=z_j(t_i)+\nu_{j,i},
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:system}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde u$ is the local concentration, $x_j$ the hidden state of cell $j$, $z_j$ its noise-free normalized ERK reporter and $y_{j,i}$ the observation at frame $t_i$ with noise $\nu_{j,i}$. Neither $\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}$ nor $\tilde u$ is calibrated here. The information $\Hist_t$ holds the executed commands and the observations that have arrived by $t$.

\emph{Reuse task.} The amplitude and duration of the next command are fixed; only its timing is decided. A previous command of $m\in\{3,30\}$~min ends at time 0, and a 5-min probe at the same amplitude acts on the receiver at $\tau\in\{0,2,\ldots,120\}$~min. With $z_j^{\tau}$ the response when the probe acts at $\tau$ and $r^{\rm nv}_j$ the same receiver's rise to the probe without a previous command,
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 S_j(\tau)&=\ind\Big\{\max_{s\in[\tau,\tau+W]}z_j^{\tau}(s)-z_j^{\tau}(\tau)\geq\kappa\,r^{\rm nv}_j\Big\},\\
 R_{j,t}&=\Prob\big(S_j(t)=1\mid\Hist_t\big),
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:task}
\end{equation}
with window $W=20$~min. Success is scored on the noise-free response; decisions see only noisy observations. The completion time is $T=\tau+W$ on success and $T_{\rm f}=140$~min on failure, the latest completion of any success. The design problem is
\begin{equation}
 \min\ \E[T\mid m]\quad\text{s.t.}\quad \Prob(S=1\mid m)\geq q,\quad m\in\{3,30\},
 \label{eq:design}
\end{equation}
with $q=0.9$ the required reliability, checked empirically on held-out samples rather than guaranteed; rules may be calibrated to higher targets to meet it (Sec.~\ref{sec:waiting}). Requiring $q$ for each previous command, not on average, prevents a rule from buying speed after one history with failures after the other. We use $\kappa=0.5$, fixed before the waiting analyses after $\kappa=0.8$ proved infeasible; a later scan over $\{0.30,0.35,\ldots,0.70\}$ shows that it is also the largest value at which the oracle still succeeds for $>99\%$ of simulated receivers within 120~min, pooled over both histories (99.25\%, 98.5\% after the 30-min command; 95.1\% at 0.55, 82.0\% at 0.60). If the receiver's state reaches the controller late, or the command takes time to arrive, a probe decided at $t$ acts at $t+d$: the rule must predict $R$ at $t+d$ from $\Hist_t$, a forecast with horizon $h=d$.

Predictors for horizons $h\in\{2,10,20,30\}$~min start after a fixed 10-min warmup; future reporters are targets only. Table~\ref{tab:features} lists the feature sets (slope: last reporter increment per minute; $\int u$: cumulative command; repository code keys P, C, O, A, M, A+M and H, in table order); every learned predictor also receives the known future command and is fitted by ridge regression with equal weight per condition. Lags are the reporter and command values of the previous 5--30 frames. The causal reporter and input filters with scales $\ell\in\{2,10,60\}$~min are
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \bar y_{\ell,i}&=e^{-\Delta_i/\ell}\,\bar y_{\ell,i-1}+(1-e^{-\Delta_i/\ell})\,y_i,\\
 v_{\ell}(t)&=\tfrac{1}{\ell}\textstyle\int_0^t e^{-(t-s)/\ell}u(s)\,ds,
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:filters}
\end{equation}
with $\bar y_{\ell,0}=y_0$ and $\Delta_i=t_i-t_{i-1}$. As mechanism we execute the authors' B3 model with their 1,000 posterior samples. With $f_s(t)$ the noise-free reporter of sample $s$ under the recorded command, the samples are weighted by the cell's past reporter,
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 w_s(t)&\propto\textstyle\exp\big[-\sum_{t_i\le t}(y_i-f_s(t_i))^2/(2\sigma^2)\big],\\
 \hat\delta_{t,h}&=\textstyle\sum_s w_s(t)\,[f_s(t+h)-f_s(t)],
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:b3}
\end{equation}
a fixed-parameter particle approximation of the B3 belief that uses the same information as the learned predictors. Since $\hat\delta_{t,h}$ already uses the command history, \pr{F+B3+Input} tests only the extra value of explicit input features. Penalty, $\sigma$ and lag length are chosen on validation data; normalization, lag filling and fitting details are in the repository.

\subsection{Calibrated Waiting Rules}
\label{sec:rules}
A waiting rule probes at the first decision time at which its readiness estimate reaches a threshold $c$, or at 120~min,
\begin{equation}
 \tau=\min\big(\{t:\widehat R_{t}\geq c\}\cup\{120\}\big),
 \label{eq:wait}
\end{equation}
a stopping time on the observations, as in sequential tests \cite{wald1945}. Three estimators of $R_t$ are compared on B3: \emph{current} uses only $y_t$, through a Gaussian kernel over the (reporter, success) states of reference receivers; \emph{smoothed} applies the same kernel to a causal exponential smoothing of the observations (scale 2--16~min); the \emph{B3 belief} weights reference receivers under the known command history by all observations, as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:b3}, with likelihood s.d.\ equal to the noise s.d.\ floored at 0.001. The comparator is one fixed wait per previous command, a history-conditioned guard interval. References are one half of the posterior samples (calibration; each receiver left out of its own references); the other half (evaluation) never enters references, thresholds or waits. Every rule, fixed or adaptive, chooses its parameters per previous command (a wait, a threshold, or for smoothing a scale and a threshold) to minimize that history's calibration completion subject to its calibration success $\geq$ a target, i.e.\ Eq.~\eqref{eq:design} on calibration samples. Twenty splits redraw the halves and the observation noise.

With delay $d$, the \emph{B3-predictive} rule averages the references' success at $t+d$ under the same weights, $\widehat R_{t+d\mid t}=\sum_s w_s(t)S_s(t+d)$; the \emph{B3 nowcast} ignores the delay. The model-free comparator, \emph{smoothing plus slope}, also extrapolates: a kernel over the smoothed level and its last increment, $(\bar y_t,\bar y_t-\bar y_{t-\Delta})$, with the covariance of smoothed white noise, maps the current trend to success at $t+d$. Observations arrive every $\Delta\in\{2,6,10\}$~min and decisions are made when they arrive.

\section{Data and Evaluation}
\label{sec:data}
All numerical files come from the paper-linked repository at a pinned commit \cite{lfnsrepo,mendeley2020}; commands follow the authors' model XML, and per-cell normalization uses only frames before stimulation onset. Table~\ref{tab:inventory} lists the 22 conditions with their roles and cell counts (1,565 cells in total, 2-min frames). Roles were frozen before any newly used response file was downloaded: Test A is a new protocol, Test B's timing was inferred from the authors' simulations, and Test C uses new concentrations recorded in the session of the inspected mixed blocks (role ``Viewed''). Because the authors fitted B3 to the sustained and 3/20 means and compared architectures on the single 5-min and mixed blocks at 2.5 and 250~ng/ml (``fit'' and ``arch.'' in the last column of Table~\ref{tab:inventory}), B3 is a calibrated reference, not a model blind to those protocols; it is executed from the authors' files without refitting (posterior-predictive mean within $8.5\times10^{-6}$ of their exports). In the corrected leave-one-protocol-out (LOPO) each of six protocol groups is held out with predictor tuning restricted to the training protocols. Forecast rows are assigned to the phases stimulation, next command (a pulse starts in $(t,t+h]$), early washout ($\le30$~min after a pulse) and late. The score is the square root of the equally weighted condition MSE,
\begin{equation}
 \mathrm{RMSE}(h)=\Big[\tfrac{1}{|\mathcal{K}|}\textstyle\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\tfrac{1}{N_{k,h}}\sum_{i\in k}(\hat y_{i+h}-y_{i+h})^2\Big]^{1/2},
 \label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
over the conditions $\mathcal{K}$ of a role. Because every error contains the reporter noise, we also report a noise-adjusted skill, $1-\overline{(\mathrm{MSE}-\hat\sigma_\nu^2)}/\overline{(\mathrm{MSE}_{\rm Persist}-\hat\sigma_\nu^2)}$, with $\hat\sigma_\nu$ a robust second-difference estimate of white reporter noise per condition (0.006--0.009), and 95\% intervals from resampling cells within conditions. Cells share a session and no day identifiers exist, so intervals describe cell sampling, not independent experiments. Every analysis was frozen in its own protocol before running, but all are post hoc with respect to inspected data.

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}
\subsection{Which Information Predicts the Response}
Table~\ref{tab:core}(a) reports the RMSE of Eq.~\eqref{eq:score} at 10~min for the validation (Val.) and test roles (A--C) of Table~\ref{tab:inventory} (last column: 2~min on Test A); bold marks the best predictor per column, and the bottom rows count the conditions in which the first predictor of each pair has the lower MSE. On Test A at 10~min, reporter filtering reduced the error by 9.1\% relative to \pr{Current}, the B3 increment by 4.5\% relative to \pr{Filter}, and input history by 1.0\% relative to \pr{Filter+B3}, giving 0.02098, 8.2\% below \pr{Persist}; these reductions use different baselines and are not additive. Across the four roles the B3 increment lowered the error on every one (4.5--9.8\%), whereas filtering helped only on the validation protocol and Test A (4.0\% and 9.1\%; none on Tests B and C, although at 2, 20 and 30~min it helped on all four). Because \pr{Current} was itself worse than \pr{Persist} on three roles, \pr{Filter} beat persistence only on Tests A and C (by 2.9\% and 1.7\%), whereas \pr{Filter+B3} beat it on all four (by 6.7--7.6\%). On the MSE scale the three Test A reductions are 17.3\%, 8.9\% and 2.0\%; subtracting the white-noise floor, under a tenth of the \pr{Persist} MSE at 10~min, raises them only to 18.8\%, 9.8\% and 2.2\%. \pr{Filter+B3} explains 15.5\% of the noise-free \pr{Persist} error (Table~\ref{tab:core}(c) gives this noise-adjusted skill by horizon, with a 95\% cell-bootstrap interval for \pr{Filter+B3}), and the cell-bootstrap intervals of the three RMSE reductions were 7.4--10.9\%, 3.0--6.1\% and 0.6--1.4\%. The input-history gain was small and not consistent across conditions (two of four on Test A; 0.4\% and 0.1\% on B and C). The lagged baseline \pr{Lag} was 2.3\% worse than \pr{Filter} on Test A, but adding the B3 increment to it still reduced the error by 6.9\% (four of four concentrations), so on Test A the mechanistic gain holds for both the filter and the lag representation of the history. In the corrected LOPO (Table~\ref{tab:core}(b), where F+B3 and F+B3+In abbreviate \pr{Filter+B3} and \pr{F+B3+Input}; bold: best per held-out protocol; last column: B3's prior use of that protocol, with $^\circ$ marking use at other concentrations), \pr{F+B3+Input} beat \pr{Filter+B3} in all six folds (by 0.1--2.0\%) and \pr{Lag+B3} beat \pr{Lag} in all six, but with the 10-min pulse held out, one of the two protocols unseen by B3, \pr{Filter+B3} was 1.1\% worse than \pr{Filter}. At longer horizons the mechanistic increment mattered more: on Test A its skill rose to 21\% at 20 and 30~min against 9--13\% for \pr{Filter}, and it anticipated the half-decay crossing of the reporter more often (balanced accuracy 57--58\% versus 52--53\%, ``Crossing BA'' in Table~\ref{tab:core}(c); 65--67\% versus 54--57\% on Test B), although no predictor did so reliably. Explicit input history became harmful on Tests A and B: \pr{F+B3+Input} fell to $-1.7\%$ skill at 20~min on Test A and to $-50\%$ and $-53\%$ on Test B at 20 and 30~min, although on Test C it lowered the 20- and 30-min error by 3.6\% and 3.0\%.

\subsection{The Next-Command Window}
By phase (Fig.~\ref{fig:prediction}), the B3 increment reduced the error of \pr{Filter} during stimulation (8.9\%), early washout (4.6\%) and the late phase (6.6\%), but raised it in the next-command window from 0.03306 to 0.03544; at 2, 20 and 30~min it lowered the next-command error (by 2.9\%, 4.0\% and 6.9\%), so the failure is specific to the 10-min horizon. That window holds two mixed conditions with ten forecast origins each, five before the 30-min pulse (20~min after the initial 3-min pulse) and five before the 5-min pulse that follows it after 60~min: the increment helped at 0.25~ng/ml (0.03652 to 0.03485) and hurt at 25~ng/ml (0.02920 to 0.03603). Two phase-gated variants fitted on the training protocols did not repair this: a switch to \pr{Filter} in next-command rows was rejected by the training data, where the increment helped (in-sample MSE $6.3\times10^{-4}$ versus $8.4\times10^{-4}$), and phase-specific weights (\pr{F+B3 (phase)} in Table~\ref{tab:core}(c)) raised the next-command error to 0.03571 (held-out mixed fold: 0.03630, versus 0.03537 for \pr{Filter+B3} and 0.03302 for \pr{Filter}). The training protocols contain no probe after a long command, so their next-command windows cannot teach the correction. The mixed-protocol probe of Sec.~\ref{sec:natural}, taken from the same 25~ng/ml cells, is consistent with B3 overestimating recovery after the long command, but only the five origins before the 5-min pulse follow that command, and the stored scores do not separate them from the other five.

\subsection{Waiting under a Per-History Reliability Requirement}
\label{sec:waiting}
Readiness and waiting were simulated on B3 with 25~ng/ml commands and observations every 2~min. Readiness depended almost only on the current reporter, waits of 6--48~min never succeeded, and no state met the criterion without the probe. In Fig.~\ref{fig:waiting}(a,b) the smoothed and B3-belief frontiers lie between the fixed waits and the oracle and nearly coincide, whereas the current-observation frontier is no better than the fixed waits after the 3-min command and better only at 90\% success or above after the 30-min command. Calibrated to 0.90, every rule, fixed waits included, met 0.9 for both histories in only 7--11 of 20 splits, so a calibration target equal to the requirement leaves no margin. At 0.94 all rules met it in every split at noise 0.005, which is why Table~\ref{tab:waiting} tabulates this target, a choice made after the splits were evaluated. The table gives, for each rule (``Current obs.'': the current estimator), the evaluation success $S_m$ (\%) and mean completion $T_m$ (min) after the $m$-min command on the primary split at the reference noise s.d.\ 0.005 (lower block: 0.01 with the same fixed waits) and, as medians over the 20 splits, the difference $\Delta T=T_{\rm rule}-T_{\rm fix}$ in mean completion $T$ over both histories (negative: earlier), the closed share $G=(T_{\rm fix}-T_{\rm rule})/(T_{\rm fix}-T_{\rm oracle})$ of the gap to the oracle, and the number of splits (Met) with $S_3,S_{30}\geq90\%$. Feedback finished 7.2~min (B3 belief) and 7.7~min (smoothed) earlier than per-history fixed waits (94 and 110~min on the primary split), faster in all 20 splits, and closed 42\% and 45\% of the gap to the oracle. At the other pre-specified targets, 0.90 and 0.92, where the requirement was met in 7--11 and 16--19 of 20 splits, feedback closed 38--42\% of the gap (saving 5.5--6.6~min), again faster in all 20 splits. The current-reporter rule closed none of it. Under a pooled requirement (success averaged over both histories), the B3 belief and smoothing closed 33\% and 38\% of the gap between pooled history-conditioned waits and the oracle (4.8 and 5.5~min; primary split). At noise s.d.\ 0.01 the B3 belief beat smoothing in all splits at every target (split medians 1.5--1.7~min; 30--35\% versus 21--26\% of the gap), whereas at 0.005 smoothing was 0.2--0.4~min faster. Larger failure penalties ($T_{\rm f}=160$--260~min, target 0.90) raised the B3 belief's gain over fixed waits from 5.5 to 5.9--8.7~min, with the smoothed and B3-belief rules still ahead of the fixed waits. Sensitivity analyses with pooled calibration (repository) agree: the gain persists at $\kappa=0.4$ and at a shared calibration margin, at $\kappa=0.6$ even the oracle fails for about 18\% of receivers, and under correlated noise the B3 belief needs a likelihood that models the correlation (median success over 20 splits 81\% with frozen thresholds and 92\% with an AR(1) likelihood, which met 0.9 in 13 of 20 splits), whereas the smoothed rule recovers by recalibration alone (19 of 20).

\subsection{Delay, Noise and Sampling: When the Mechanism Pays Off}
With delay, readiness must be predicted: the B3 nowcast became slower than the fixed waits from $d=20$~min on (124--138~min), whereas both predictive rules lost only about 3~min between $d=0$ and 40~min (Fig.~\ref{fig:waiting}(c)), so the value of feedback over fixed waits shrank from 5.5--5.9 to 2.2--2.6~min. The condition map (Fig.~\ref{fig:waiting}(d)) answers when the model is needed. At noise s.d.\ 0.01 the B3-predictive rule was faster than smoothing plus slope in every split of every cell, by 0.9--5.5~min, and with sampling every 6 or 10~min the model-free rule became slower than the fixed waits (by 0.5--4.7~min) while the B3 rule still beat them. At the reference noise the model helped with 10-min sampling (0.3--1.1~min) and in two of five delays with 6-min sampling; with 2-min sampling and at noise 0.0025 the smoothed trend was 0.1--1.6~min faster, and at low noise the B3 belief lost reliability as its weights collapsed (median success 86.5--88.0\% with 2-min sampling). The model was faster in at least 16 of 20 splits in 22 of the 45 map cells and also no more than 0.5 points below the smoothing-plus-slope rule's median success in 18 of them (bold in Fig.~\ref{fig:waiting}(d)); the other four, all at noise 0.01, are not bold only because the B3-predictive rule's median success was 0.6--1.7 points lower. The rules of the map (fixed waits, B3-predictive, smoothing plus slope) are calibrated to 0.90 per history, at which, as in Sec.~\ref{sec:waiting}, the requirement was met in only part of the splits (8, 0--11 and 4--14 of 20 per map cell), so the map compares rules near rather than safely above 0.9; its success condition, on pooled median success, limits but does not rule out trading reliability for speed after one history.

\subsection{Natural Probes in Measured Cells}
\label{sec:natural}
Two recorded protocols contain later pulses that act as probes at fixed waits (25~ng/ml): in the 3/20 protocol each 3-min pulse comes 20~min after the previous one, with the cell's own first-pulse rise as naive reference (58 cells); in the mixed protocol (84 cells) a 5-min pulse comes 60~min after a 30-min pulse, scored against single 5-min pulse cells (92 cells). Both probes were early by B3's standard: B3 predicted no success for any sample, and every estimator called no cell ready. The measured cells agreed: 19\% met the criterion at the first 3/20 probe and 3--9\% at later ones, and none at the mixed probe, so the rules made no false ready call but missed the 19\%. The rises differed, however: at the mixed probe measured cells reached a median 4\% of the naive rise where B3 predicts 31\%, consistent with B3 overestimating recovery after a long command. These cells also supply the 25~ng/ml next-command window, but only half of its origins precede this probe, so the probe neither locates nor independently confirms that error. After a short pulse B3 erred the other way: at the first 3/20 probe it underestimated the rise (8\% versus 16\% of the naive rise). The B3 weights collapsed on measured cells (effective sample size 1.0--1.4), so a real-data filter needs process noise or cell heterogeneity, and the measured noise floor (0.007--0.008) lies between the map's reference and high noise, where with 2-min sampling the map turns from the smoothed trend to the model, which would help only if it were right.

\subsection{Implications for MC Design}
A previous command acts on the next one like ISI whose memory sits in the receiver's biochemistry rather than only in the channel \cite{jamali2019,tepekule2015}. A fixed wait is a guard interval; conditioning it on the known previous command is the first step (94 and 110~min after the 3- and 30-min commands at target 0.94, primary split), and requiring reliability for each previous command rather than on average lengthens the guard after the long command (106 instead of 102~min at target 0.90, primary split). Feedback then adapts to state variability within each history, much as adaptive detection adapts to residual interference \cite{damrath2016,mosayebi2014}, and recovers about 40\% of the gap to the oracle at the reference noise. Which estimator feedback needs depends on the noise, the sampling interval and the loop delay: a smoothed reporter with its trend suffices for clean feedback and for frequent feedback at the reference noise, whereas a model is worth its cost for noisy feedback and for sparse feedback at the reference noise, provided it is right about recovery; with a loop delay, either estimate must be predictive. A prospective test should randomize 3- versus 30-min preconditioning per chamber, probe at 60--120~min, measure the naive response in same-session controls and count sessions rather than cells.

\subsection{Evidence Boundaries}
\emph{Data.} The prediction comparisons are post hoc on inspected roles; in the frozen analysis of the main fresh test, history features alone improved on persistence by only 0.7\%; Test C shares a session with inspected data, no experimental-day identifiers exist, protocol generalization is therefore not cross-day generalization, and B3 had seen some protocols; the noise floor assumes white reporter noise. \emph{Model.} Waiting results come from one simulated model structure whose posterior samples express parameter uncertainty, not validated cell heterogeneity; the 20 splits reuse one finite posterior set; the B3 rules share the plant's structure, which favors them; the targets have no coverage guarantee and a calibration margin is not one. \emph{Task.} $\kappa=0.5$ was adopted after $\kappa=0.8$ proved infeasible; the naive response is a simulation counterfactual; amplitude and duration were fixed; the natural probes cover one dose at two fixed waits and score readiness calls, not completion time; the experimental predictors were not tested as decision models, so forecast gains do not imply better waiting decisions.

\section{Conclusion}
On experimental FGF2--ERK trajectories, a fixed calibrated mechanistic increment lowered the 10-min forecast error on the validation and all three test protocols and in five of six held-out protocols, reporter filtering on two of the four, and explicit input history added little; the increment raised the pooled 10-min error in the next-command windows of two test conditions, through the 25~ng/ml condition, and a later probe pulse in those cells is consistent with B3 overestimating recovery after a long command. In B3 simulations with a reliability requirement for each previous command, waiting on a smoothed reporter or the B3 belief closes about 40\% of the gap between history-conditioned guard intervals and the oracle at the reference noise; a smoothed reporter with its trend suffices for clean feedback and for frequent feedback at the reference noise, a correct mechanistic model pays off for noisy feedback and for sparse feedback at the reference noise, and with a feedback delay of about 20~min or more, readiness must be predicted rather than nowcast.
